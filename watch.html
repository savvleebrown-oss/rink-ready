Where to watch

Three ways to watch, sorted by effort.

You do not need a cable package, and you do not have to commit to a season. Almost all the hockey a new fan can watch is either local, free, or costs about as much as a takeout dinner.

Pick the one that fits your week

None of these is the "right" way in. The best first game is the one you can actually get to.

In person

Go to a rink

Cedar Hollow Ice Barn and Riverside Twin Rinks both host midweek league games. Tickets run between nine and sixteen dollars at the door, the building seats under two thousand people, and the sound of skates on ice arrives before you find your seat.

Rinks are cold. Bring a coat, not a jacket. Sit on the side the players shoot toward twice, and you will see more goals.

Doors open 45 minutes before the puck drops.

Streaming

Watch on a laptop

Puckline carries every Northern Lights league game live for seven dollars a month, with replays available for a week. Icebox TV carries college and tournament hockey and has a free tier with ads if you want to try the sport out before paying for anything.

If you are watching alone, turn the commentary down for one period and just listen to the rink. It is a different game.

Puckline has a week-long replay window; local games are blacked out live on the cheapest plan.

Watch party

Find a room full of people

The Blue Line Diner on Third shows the Friday game with the sound on and a printed roster on every table. Order the soup, sit near the back, and the table of regulars will explain any call you ask about — they have been waiting all week for someone to ask.

One screen, one game, and the good seats go by six.

How to watch when you are still learning

Five things that make a first season far less baffling than watching the puck for sixty minutes.

  1. Pick one player and follow only her for a shift. About forty-five seconds. You will learn more about the shape of the game in one shift than in a whole period of chasing the puck.
  2. Watch the defender on the far side of the puck. She is usually the player telling you where the play is about to go, because she has to cover the pass nobody has made yet.
  3. Learn the two sounds. A stick hitting the ice is a pass or a shot; the crowd's sudden quiet "ohh" is a near miss. After a period you will hear the difference without looking up.
  4. Ignore the scoreboard for one period. Count completed passes instead. Hockey is mostly a passing game interrupted by whistles, and the score tells you almost nothing about who is playing well.
  5. Keep the rules page open in another tab. For weeks, offside and icing will be the only two calls you need. Both are explained in one paragraph each on our rules page.

If you are watching on a phone

  • Turn the screen sideways. Landscape is the difference between following a play and guessing at it.
  • Leave the commentary on for your first few games. It is repetitive on purpose, and the repetition is how the vocabulary sticks.
  • Use the stream's own replay button when a goal happens. A live goal is genuinely hard to read the first time.
  • Do not try to watch while doing something else. Hockey rewards attention in short bursts — watch a shift, look away, come back.

If you only watch one game this month

Make it Friday, November 20 at Twin Rinks. It is the first game of the Lantern Cup, the Owls have the best goaltender in the league, and the Foxes will spend sixty minutes trying to prove that does not matter. Tickets are fifteen dollars, the building holds twenty-two hundred people, and the last time these two met in November it went to a shootout.

If you cannot get there, the diner shows it with the sound on, and we will have a plain-language recap up by Saturday morning.

The first game I watched on a screen, I had the sound off and the rules page open. I learned more in twenty minutes than in a season of highlight clips.

Priya Raman, viewing editor
