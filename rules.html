The game in sixty seconds

If you only have a minute before the puck drops, this is the minute to spend.

Two teams, six players each: five skaters and a goaltender. They play three periods of twenty minutes, and the clock stops for every whistle, so a game takes about two and a half hours in the building even though only sixty minutes of hockey get played. The team with more goals at the end wins. If it is tied, they play a five-minute sudden-death overtime, and if that settles nothing, a shootout.

You score by putting a six-ounce rubber puck into a net that is four feet wide and six feet tall. That is a small target at speed, which is why most goals come from a scramble in front of it rather than a clean shot from distance. Players change on the fly, mid-play, in shifts of about forty-five seconds, which is why the bench always looks like a fire drill.

Three periods
Twenty minutes each, with a seventeen-minute break between the second and third. Teams switch ends after the second period.
Six on six
Five skaters plus a goalie per side, with up to eighteen skaters dressed and available to rotate through the game.
The puck
One inch thick, six ounces, frozen before the game so it slides instead of bouncing. It travels faster than a car on the highway.
The net
Four feet wide by six feet tall, with a red frame and a mesh back. The goal counts only when the whole puck crosses the goal line.

The five calls that confuse everyone first

These five account for almost every "wait, what just happened?" in a first season of watching.

Offside
The attacking team cannot enter the offensive zone before the puck does. If a player's skates are across the blue line while the puck is still outside it, play stops and the other team gets the puck outside the zone.
Icing
If a team shoots the puck from behind the center line all the way past the other team's goal line without anyone touching it, play comes back. It is a stalling rule: you cannot just fire the puck away to waste time.
Minor penalty
Most penalties are two minutes in the box. The penalized player sits, her team plays one skater short, and if the other team scores during those two minutes, the penalty ends early.
Power play
The five-on-four advantage a team gets while the other team is short-handed. Watch the passing speed up immediately — a good power play moves the puck faster than the penalty kill can chase it.
Goalie crease
The blue semicircle in front of the net. The goalie is protected inside it; an attacking player standing in it who interferes with a save will have a goal waved off.

Offside is the rule that makes new fans feel stupid, and it is genuinely simple once you watch the blue line instead of the puck.

Dani Okafor, rules editor

The rink, labelled

An NHL-sized rink is 200 feet long and 85 feet wide. The one below is drawn to be understood, not to scale.

Goal line
The thin red line the puck must fully cross for a goal. It sits eleven feet in from the end boards.
Blue line
Marks the edge of each team's offensive zone. All offside calls are decided at this line, which is why it is painted thick.
Center red line
Splits the ice in half. Icing is judged from here: a shot from behind it that crosses the far goal line can be called back.
Neutral zone
The strip of ice between the two blue lines. Most turnovers happen here, and most coaches stand exactly here too.
Face-off circle
Five circles, one center and four in the end zones. Play restarts with a face-off inside the circle nearest to where the whistle happened.
Goal crease
The blue semicircle in front of the net. It belongs to the goalie, and standing in it comes with consequences.

Who stands where

Six jobs on the ice, and one of them is allowed to fall over on purpose.

Center

The one in the middle

Takes most face-offs, leads the forecheck, and is usually the first forward back into her own zone. Centers are often the playmakers: they touch the puck more than anyone else.

Watch for: a center slowing down before a pass to buy herself half a second.

Wings

The ones on the edges

A left wing and a right wing, who patrol the boards and are expected to score. Their job is to get open, and then to be the first player in on the forecheck when the puck goes behind the net.

Watch for: a winger drifting into the slot, alone, waiting.

Defense

The ones who stay home

Two per team, one on each side. They hold the blue line, block shots, and start the breakout. A good defender is usually the player quietly making the pass that led to the goal.

Watch for: a defender stepping up at the blue line to keep a play alive.

Goaltender

The one in the net

Stops the puck, reads the play, and is the only player allowed to cover it with her hand in the crease. Goalies are also the loudest people on the ice, because they can see everything.

Watch for: a goalie tapping her stick to tell the team where the puck is going.

Penalties, and how long you sit

The referee raises an arm to signal a delayed penalty, and blows the whistle when the offending team touches the puck. Then somebody sits down.

Standard calls in the women's game.
Call Time What actually happened
Tripping 2 minutes A stick, knee or skate takes out an opponent's legs. Often accidental, always called.
Hooking 2 minutes A stick pulls an opponent back or spins her around. If it stopped a scoring chance, it can be a penalty shot instead.
Slashing 2 minutes A swing of the stick at an opponent's hands, arms or body. Referees call it by the sound as much as the sight.
High-sticking 2 or 4 minutes The stick above shoulder height makes contact. Four minutes if it draws blood.
Interference 2 minutes Hitting or blocking a player who does not have the puck. You may skate through a lane; you may not take the player out of it.
Body checking 2 minutes In the women's game, delivering a check is not allowed. Contact is part of the sport; a full check is not.
Roughing 2 minutes Post-whistle pushing that goes past shoving. Both teams usually get one.
Delay of game 2 minutes Most often shooting the puck out of play on purpose, or knocking a net off its pegs.

A major penalty is five minutes and does not end early if the other team scores. A misconduct is ten minutes and does not leave the team short-handed.

Why a win is worth two points

Standings in hockey do not count wins alone, which is why a record like 8-2-1-0 makes sense to a coach and nobody else.

A regulation win is two points and a regulation loss is zero. Lose in overtime or a shootout and you still get one point, because you forced the game past sixty minutes. Win in overtime or a shootout and you get two, but the game is recorded in a separate column. That is what the four numbers mean: wins, regulation losses, overtime losses and shootout losses.

Regulation win
Two points. The cleanest result on the table and the one coaches talk about all season.
Overtime or shootout loss
One point. Frustrating in the room, useful in March.
Regulation loss
Zero points. Sixteen of these and a club starts having conversations about next year.
Goal differential
Goals scored minus goals allowed. It is the tiebreaker nobody explains until the last week of the season.

Five things people get wrong

  • You can stop the puck with your skate and even redirect it, but you cannot kick it into the net on purpose.
  • A player is not offside if the puck is already in the zone, no matter where her skates are.
  • Icing is waved off if the other team could have played the puck before it crossed the goal line.
  • Hand passes are legal in your own defensive zone and illegal everywhere else.
  • Pulling the goalie is not a penalty or a surrender. It is a coach trading the net for a sixth skater when she needs a goal.

Glossary

Ten words you will hear in the first period. Keep this open in another tab during a broadcast.

Backcheck
Skating back toward your own net to defend, usually right after a turnover.
Forecheck
Pressuring the other team in their own zone, trying to force a mistake before they get out.
Breakout
The sequence a team uses to move the puck out of its own zone. Coaches draw these on whiteboards for a living.
Slot
The patch of ice between the face-off circles, in front of the net. Where goals come from.
Deke
A fake with the body or stick that makes a defender commit one way while the puck goes the other.
Five-hole
The gap between a goalie's pads. Useful to know because everyone behind you will yell it.
Top shelf
A shot into the upper corner of the net, above the goalie's shoulder. The crowd noise is different for these.
Power play
The five-on-four advantage after an opposing penalty. Also called the man advantage.
Penalty kill
The short-handed unit that tries to survive those two minutes without giving up a goal.
Hat trick
Three goals by one player in a game. In most buildings, the hats end up on the ice.
